# Voice and Style

Read at the start of Step 03 (writing copy) and again during Step 05 (audit). Every rule here gets enforced in audit.

---

## The voice you are writing in

Local service business website copy. The reader is a customer with a problem who wants to call someone they can trust. The voice is the voice of a competent neighbor who takes the work seriously and explains it clearly. Not a corporate marketing department. Not a chatbot. Not a startup founder pitching VCs.

**Useful frame:** Imagine the owner of the business is reading every sentence over your shoulder. Would they nod, or would they say "that's not how we talk"?

---

## The reader is the subject

The visitor is a customer with a problem. They did not come here to learn about the business. They came here to find out whether calling this number will solve their problem today. Every piece of customer-facing copy keeps them in frame.

**Customer-POV** (default). Starts with or speaks to the visitor's state. "Got a leak?" "Stop the damage today." "A pro who picks up the phone."

**Brand-POV** (exception). Describes the business. "The pro other pros recommend." "The metro's most-reviewed shop." "12 years serving the county."

Write customer-POV first. Use brand-POV only when the business fact is so specific and so load-bearing that it outperforms the customer frame. Rare cases: peer endorsements, unusual longevity, a singular credential. When in doubt, customer-POV.

**The job-to-be-done test.** Before you finalize a headline, ask: does this answer the visitor's search-intent question? ("Can someone fix this today?") If the headline makes the visitor think about the business before it tells them whether their problem gets solved, it is brand-POV. Rewrite.

### Headline examples

| Brand-POV (avoid as default) | Customer-POV (reach for first) |
| :-- | :-- |
| The Pro Other Pros Recommend | Got a problem? We're on it today. |
| The Newest Licensed Pro in Town | Same-day service in your area. |
| 12 Years of Code-Correct Work | Done right the first time. |
| The Metro's Most-Reviewed Shop | Something not working? Call us. |
| Family-Owned Since 1998 | Shows up when we say we will. |

Neither column is wrong. The right column is the default. The left column is a tool to reach for when the evidence is strong enough to earn the lead.

**Eight-word cap.** Every headline candidate is 8 words or fewer. Hyphenated compounds count as one word. The page's job is to funnel a panicked or hurried visitor to the phone. Heroes that demand reading lose them.

## Hard prohibitions

### No em dashes

Anywhere. Use commas, periods, semicolons, or "and" instead.

- Bad: "We respond same-day, often within hours, no exceptions."
- Good: "We respond same-day, often within hours. No exceptions."
- Bad: "The pro who answers the phone, same one who shows up, no dispatchers."
- Good: "The pro who answers the phone is the same one who shows up. No dispatchers."

### No real names outside testimonials

Owner names, employee names, technician names, and any other real person's first or last name must not appear in headlines, subheadlines, about prose, service descriptions, process steps, FAQ questions or answers, section headers, CTAs, or contact blocks.

**Allowed:** Brand names. If the brand contains a person's name ("Jake's Plumbing"), use the full brand phrase. Never shorten to the bare first name in body copy.

**Allowed:** Testimonial author attributes and names inside verbatim review quotes. Reviews stay as the customer wrote them.

**Rewrite patterns when the dossier names someone:**

| Instead of | Use |
| :-- | :-- |
| "Taylor answers the phone." | "You'll get a pro on the phone, not a dispatcher." |
| "Mike runs the crew." | "The owner-operator runs every job." |
| "Call Taylor." | "Call now." / "Call us." |
| "Send Jake a Note" | "Send a Note" / "Request a Visit" |
| "What Taylor Fixes" (section header) | "What We Fix" / "Calls We Take" |
| "Taylor trained in the trade." | "The owner trained in the trade." |

The business's service experience is the subject, not the person delivering it. This rule compounds the customer-POV default: names in body copy make the business the subject; removing them returns the focus to the work and the visitor.

**Why now.** Prospect-spec sites are built before the owner has signed a contract or confirmed name usage. Names in prominent copy create legal, personnel, and longevity risk (owner retires, sells, dies; employee leaves; partner buys out). Generic phrasing survives those changes without rewrites.

### Contractions only

Website copy always contracts. "You have" becomes "you've." "Do not" becomes "don't." "We are" becomes "we're." "It is" becomes "it's." "Cannot" becomes "can't." "Will not" becomes "won't." Uncontracted forms read as stiff and corporate; contractions are how the owner actually speaks.

Applies to every customer-facing block: `headline-candidates`, `about-prose`, `service`, `process-step` (both titles and body), `testimonial` (only when rewriting your own copy — never rewrite a review quote), `faq`, `cta`, `section-header`, `contact`. Does NOT apply to `business-summary` (internal prose) or `seo` (title tag and meta description, where clarity beats voice).

- Bad: "slow problems you have been putting off"
- Good: "slow problems you've been putting off"
- Bad: "we will tell you how soon we can be there"
- Good: "we'll tell you how soon we can be there"
- Bad: "a problem that will not stop"
- Good: "a problem that won't stop"
- Bad: "a leak you cannot find"
- Good: "a leak you can't find"
- Bad: "the owner is on the truck"
- Good: "the owner's on the truck"

Exceptions, narrow:
- Do not force an awkward contraction.
- Keep possessives uncontracted when they collide with a verb contraction ("The owner's truck is outside" stays; do not re-contract the verb).
- Emphasis: in the rare case a sentence needs force, uncontracted is allowed if the surrounding copy makes it deliberate. Default is still to contract.

### Banned words

These read as AI-generated. Never use them.

- delve, leverage, robust, comprehensive, cutting-edge, streamline
- unlock, elevate, empower, seamless, navigate (as a verb for abstract concepts)
- landscape (as a metaphor for an industry or market)
- innovative, transformative, revolutionary, disruptive
- holistic, synergy, ecosystem (when not literally about ecosystems)

### Banned openers

These are AI tells. Never start a paragraph or section with them.

- "In today's fast-paced world..."
- "It's worth noting that..."
- "When it comes to..."
- "In the world of..."
- "Imagine a..."
- "More than ever..."
- "At the end of the day..."

### Banned phrases

- "Trusted by thousands" (vague, fake-feeling)
- "Industry-leading" (cliché)
- "Best in class" (cliché)
- "Second to none" (cliché)
- "Unparalleled" (cliché)
- "Tailor-made solutions" (vague AI-speak)

## Patterns to use

### One clear idea per paragraph

If a paragraph contains two ideas, split it. Three-sentence maximum for any paragraph anywhere on the site.

### Specific beats general

Numbers, names, places, and concrete details signal real writing. Generic claims signal AI.

- Bad: "Our experienced team handles all your needs."
- Good: "A licensed pro shows up the same day you call, diagnoses the problem on site, and walks you through what they find."

### Blended voice

Use "we" for actions ("we respond same-day"). Use the business name for establishing moments ("Example Services serves..."). Avoid third-person passive constructions ("Example Services offers a wide range of services").

### Self-contained FAQ answers

Each FAQ answer must make sense as a standalone passage. AI search engines extract answers, not Q+A pairs. Lead with the actual answer in the first sentence.

- Bad: "Yes, we are."
- Good: "Yes. The business holds the trade license required to perform this work in the state, and carries the required bond and insurance."

### Section headers read as labels, not sentences

Section headers (`{topic: section-header, for: ...}` blocks) are the H2s the builder drops above the services grid, about prose, process list, FAQ, and contact form. They are NOT places to restate the business's pitch in a full sentence.

- **Max 5 words. Hard ceiling.** Shorter is better. 2-3 words is often strongest.
- **No terminal punctuation.** No periods, no question marks, no exclamation points.
- **Short, impactful, creative.** Headers earn a spot on the page. Generic ("Our Services", "About Us", "Contact") fails. Punchy and specific wins.
- **Specific to this business.** Swap-test every candidate. If it fits a competitor's site unchanged, rewrite.
- **Not meta-descriptive.** Do not narrate the existence of the section.
- **Not an instruction.** Step titles belong on steps. Section headers frame the section.
- **Contractions still apply.** "What We Do" is fine; "What We're Known For" contracts.

Bad (every one of these reads as narration rather than curated headlines):
- "Urgent repairs and the slow problems you have been putting off, handled the same way." (full sentence, narrative)
- "One pro, one phone, one town, and the neighbors to take care of." (poetic description)
- "Short answers to the things worth knowing before the phone rings." (meta-descriptive)
- "The phone is the fastest way. The form is for when you cannot talk right now." (two sentences in an H2)

Good:
- Services: "What We Fix", "Calls We Take", "Jobs We Show Up For"
- About: "The Shop", "Owner on the Line", "One Pro, On Call"
- Process: "How It Goes", "Phone to Fixed", "What Happens Next"
- Testimonials: "What Neighbors Say", "Same Story, Every Time", "From Our Customers"
- FAQ: "Before You Call", "Usual Questions", "Things People Ask First"
- Contact: "Start the Call", "Reach Out", "Call or Visit"

If a header genuinely needs context, write a separate `Subhead direction:` line under the candidate list. Subheads can be full sentences (still contracted); headers cannot.

## The review-count rule

Review counts and star ratings are visual trust signals. The builder renders them as review-badge style stat elements. They never appear in prose anywhere.

**Never write:**
- "With over 100 five-star reviews..."
- "Our 4.6-star average..."
- "Trusted by 1,800+ homeowners..."
- "Hundreds of happy customers agree..."

**Instead write what the reviews mean, not what they are:**
- "Customers describe a team that shows up on time, explains what they find, and cleans up when they're done."
- "Homeowners across the metro keep calling us back."
- "We are the pro your neighbors recommend by name."

The review count and star rating live ONLY inside `{topic: stat, ...}` blocks in the content package.

## Voice tone variations

Pick one in Step 02 (Strategy). The tone influences word choice and rhythm but never the hard prohibitions above.

### Conversational-Confident
Used when the business has earned its reputation and does not need to prove it. Short declarative sentences. "We" voice. Specific details from reviews. No hedging.

### Warm-Professional
Used when the brand wants approachability with credibility. Slightly longer sentences. Uses "we" and the business name interchangeably. Acknowledges the customer's situation before launching into capabilities.

### Gritty-Friendly
Used for hands-on service brands serving working-class neighborhoods. Plain language, no jargon, occasionally colloquial. "Show up when we say we will" rather than "demonstrate punctuality and reliability."

### Executive-Authoritative
Used for B2B services or premium-positioned brands courting larger accounts. Slightly more formal. Stats and credentials prominent. Avoids overly casual phrasing.

### Emergency-Calming
Used for emergency-first businesses (24/7 service, restoration, locksmiths). Direct, action-oriented. Reassuring rather than urgent. The visitor is already panicked, the copy should not amplify it.

---

## When to break a rule

The prohibitions are absolute. The patterns are defaults.

If a specific detail from the dossier sounds awkward but is true and specific, keep the awkwardness. Polish that erases specificity is the wrong trade.

If the business name is itself the rule violation (e.g., a business called "Cutting Edge Auto Repair"), use the name as written but do not echo the cliché elsewhere.

If a quote in a testimonial contains an em dash in the original review, replace it with a comma when transcribing. Reviews are raw, your output is not.
